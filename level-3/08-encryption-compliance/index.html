    08 · Encryption, Key Management & Compliance¶

    Security reviews of a Snowflake deployment almost always ask the same questions. Is the data encrypted? With whose keys? How are keys rotated? Can we revoke Snowflake's access? Which compliance programmes does it support? Most of the answers are "Snowflake does it by default", and a few depend on your edition and on choices you make. This lesson collects what Snowflake's documentation says, so you can answer accurately rather than from memory.

    Encrypted by default, end to end¶

    From Snowflake's documentation on end-to-end encryption:

    • All customer data at rest is encrypted, and data is encrypted in transit (TLS) to and from the Snowflake service. There's nothing to turn on.
    • Data loaded into tables is stored in Snowflake's file format, encrypted. When it's processed, it's decrypted for the operation and re-encrypted afterwards.
    • Files in internal stages are encrypted by Snowflake automatically, on upload by the client.
    • Files in external stages (your own bucket) are your storage, encrypted as your bucket is configured. Snowflake recommends client-side encryption for them, and supports it for loading and unloading with a stage-level MASTER_KEY (a 128- or 256-bit AES key, Base64-encoded), or with the cloud provider's server-side encryption options:
    CREATE STAGE learn_db.raw.s3_encrypted
  URL = 's3://my-company-landing/encrypted/'
  STORAGE_INTEGRATION = s3_landing_int
  ENCRYPTION = (TYPE = 'AWS_SSE_KMS' KMS_KEY_ID = 'arn:aws:kms:eu-west-1:123456789012:key/1a2b3c4d-0000-0000-0000-000000000000');
    • Results unloaded to a stage are encrypted per the stage's settings.

    The key hierarchy¶

    Snowflake's documentation describes a hierarchical key model with four levels, each encrypting ("wrapping") the keys below it:

    root key            (in a cloud-provider hardware security module, HSM)
 └─ account master key    (one hierarchy per customer account)
     └─ table master key  (per table)
         └─ file keys     (per micro-partition file)

    Two consequences matter. Every account has its own hierarchy, so one customer's keys never protect another's data. And each key protects only a small amount of data, which limits the impact of any single key and makes rotation practical.

    Rotation vs rekeying¶

    These are different operations, and security questionnaires often confuse them:

    Key rotation Periodic rekeying
    What happens keys older than 30 days are retired, and new keys are created for new data data protected by a retired key older than one year is re-encrypted with a new key
    Old key kept, used only to decrypt existing data, destroyed when no longer needed destroyed once its data has been re-encrypted
    Edition all Enterprise and higher, enabled by ACCOUNTADMIN
    Cost to you none Snowflake documents a possible additional storage charge during the transition 
    ALTER ACCOUNT SET PERIODIC_DATA_REKEYING = TRUE;     -- Enterprise+, ACCOUNTADMIN

    Rotation is cheap because creating a new key affects only data written afterwards. Rekeying is a real rewrite of stored data, which is why it runs on a yearly cycle, and why a policy that demands "all data re-encrypted under a fresh key regularly" is a rekeying requirement, not a rotation one.

    Tri-Secret Secure: your key in the chain¶

    On Business Critical Edition and above, Tri-Secret Secure combines a Snowflake-maintained key with a customer-managed key held in your cloud provider's key management service (AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault or Google Cloud KMS) into a composite master key that protects your data. The practical effect: if you revoke Snowflake's access to your key, Snowflake can no longer decrypt your data. That's a strong control for regulated data, and also an availability risk. A mistaken key-policy change or a deleted key makes your data unreadable, by you as well. Enabling it is a coordinated process involving Snowflake Support and your key-management team. It wasn't run for this course, so follow the current documentation step by step.

    Compliance¶

    Snowflake publishes its certifications and attestations (such as SOC reports, ISO certifications and PCI DSS) and supports regulated workloads (such as HIPAA) on qualifying editions and regions. The current list and its conditions live on Snowflake's Trust Center and compliance pages, and they change, so this course doesn't reproduce it. What matters for practitioners is the shared responsibility split:

    Snowflake is responsible for You are responsible for
    encryption at rest and in transit, key hierarchy, rotation choosing the edition and region your obligations require
    physical and infrastructure security, service availability users, roles, MFA and authentication policies (lessons 01–02)
    providing audit data (access history, login history) network policies and private connectivity (lesson 03)
    classifying and masking sensitive data (lessons 04–05)
    reviewing and retaining audit logs (next lesson)
    external stage buckets and their encryption

    An auditor asking "is personal data protected?" will want evidence from your side of that table: who can read the customers table, who did read it last month, and how you know.

    How It Actually Works¶

    Each micro-partition file is encrypted with its own file key. The file key is stored only in encrypted form, wrapped by the table's master key, which is wrapped by the account master key, which is wrapped by the root key in the HSM. To read a file, the service unwraps the chain from the top, which requires the HSM. That's why keys never leave hardware protection in plain form at the root. Rotating a key means new data gets wrapped under a new key, while old wrapped keys remain readable by their retired parent until they're no longer needed. Rekeying re-encrypts the affected data files themselves under fresh keys, so it's proportional to data volume.

    With Tri-Secret Secure, the account-level key material depends on both Snowflake's key and the key in your KMS. Snowflake must call your KMS to obtain it, so revoking that permission breaks the chain at the account level, and every file below becomes undecryptable.

    Common mistakes¶

    • Unencrypted external stage buckets because "Snowflake encrypts everything". Your bucket is your responsibility.
    • Confusing rotation with rekeying in policy documents and questionnaires.
    • Promising customer-managed keys on Standard or Enterprise. Tri-Secret Secure requires Business Critical or higher.
    • Tri-Secret Secure without key-lifecycle discipline. A deleted or mis-scoped key is a self-inflicted outage.
    • Copying a compliance list from a blog post. Check Snowflake's current Trust Center pages for your edition and region.

    Exercise¶

    1. Write the one-page answer to a customer's security questionnaire section on encryption for your account: at rest, in transit, key ownership, rotation, rekeying, and external stages. Cite the documentation pages you used.
    2. Check whether periodic rekeying is enabled in your account (SHOW PARAMETERS LIKE 'PERIODIC_DATA_REKEYING' IN ACCOUNT), and decide whether it should be.
    3. Configure an external stage on a bucket with server-side KMS encryption, load the course's order files through it, and confirm that loading still works when the bucket requires encryption.
    4. List the controls from lessons 01–07 that an auditor would ask you to evidence for a table containing personal data, and the query or screen you would show for each.
